Research Focus

Reuniting crops with their ancestral microbiome

Dario's research centers on plant-microbe interactions and the microbiome, with a particular focus on stress-induced microbiome assembly in Solanaceae, across both wild and domesticated species. His core thesis: plant domestication severed a millennia-old alliance between crops and the soil microbes that protect them.

Working within the Dutch MiCRop project, he applies the centre of origin hypothesis — the idea that plants co-evolved with their microbiomes in their birthplace — to develop microbiome-based strategies for disease resistance in agriculture.

PhD Research Highlight

The lost microbiome of the potato

For his doctoral research, Dario traveled over 2,000 km through the Ecuadorian highlands, collecting soil from 14 paired sites, each with an adjacent wild and cultivated potato plot. He transported 200 kg of soil per location to a greenhouse in Quito, where, in a common garden experiment, he challenged potato plants with Phytophthora infestans, the same pathogen that caused the Irish famine.

Plants in wild soil consistently showed fewer symptoms. When the soil was heat-sterilized, the protection vanished, confirming the microbiome as the key factor. DNA sequencing revealed ~150 microbial species consistently absent from agricultural soils. From those, around 60 bacterial candidates were associated with protection, and 14 were lab-cultivated and assembled into a synthetic bacterial community.

Adding these 14-member native synthetic community to the agricultural soil restored approximately 70% of the disease resistance observed in natural soils — a promising proof of concept for microbiome rewilding in agriculture.

— PhD thesis, Utrecht University, defended March 19, 2026

The long-term vision extends beyond potato: peppers, tomatoes, and cacao, also Ecuadorian in origin, are next in line. "This is a safer way to make potatoes stronger than developing new pesticides," Dario has said.

Background

From beer quality to wild potato microbiomes

Dario's research career began in Ecuador with hands-on roles spanning industry, teaching, and academic research. After graduating Magna Cum Laude in Biotechnology from USFQ, he worked as a Quality Analyst at Ecuador's largest brewery Cervecería Nacional (2012–2013), managing microbial quality assessment and leading tasting panels, before moving into pharmaceutical R&D as an Expert Analyst at ENFARMA E.P. (2014–2016), Ecuador's public pharmaceutical company.

Parallel to these roles, he built a strong foundation in plant science as a Lab Technician at USFQ's Agronomy & Food Biotechnology Lab, a Science Teacher at Colegio Menor San Francisco de Quito, and a Researcher at Brown Breeding, working on UV-C and elicitor-based disease control in roses. Earlier still, he contributed to USFQ's Plant Biotechnology Lab and the Herbarium QUSF, gathering and identifying native Ecuadorian plant species, and propagating them by in vitro culture.

A pivotal moment came when a paper on synthetic microbial communities sparked a new direction. He joined the Dangl Lab at UNC Chapel Hill as a Visiting Researcher (2018–2019), studying low-dose glyphosate effects and synthetic bacterial communities on Arabidopsis, and received training in genomics and metagenomics bioinformatics. During this time, he was also completing his MSc in Microbiology at USFQ, which, combined with his research experience, led directly to his PhD at Utrecht University and NIOO-KNAW.
